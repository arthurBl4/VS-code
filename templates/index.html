Does violence at the margins of protests stem from the claims being made, or from national policing traditions?

Two hypotheses are put to the test: the nature of the demands carried by the crowd, or the public-order tradition specific to each country. The charts below settle the question with numbers.

Crowd dynamics and public-order doctrines

By cross-referencing thousands of events recorded by ACLED, this dashboard isolates the impact of national policing traditions against purely thematic readings of social conflict.

H1: the effect of claims

Some causes (climate, Gaza, pensions, agriculture) would attract more radical crowds or counter-demonstrators, and therefore more violence, whatever the country.

H2: the effect of policing traditions

According to della Porta and Reiter, an “escalated force” style of policing provokes clashes that “negotiated management” avoids. Violence would then depend on the country, not on the cause.

Method and limitations

Definitions. “Repression” = police interventions + excessive force against protesters. “Violence” = riots (“Violent demonstration”) and mob violence (“Mob violence”). Every rate uses the country’s total number of events as its denominator, which makes them comparable.

Typology of traditions. Grouping countries into families is indicative, inspired by della Porta and Reiter (negotiated policing versus escalated force). It is debatable, notably for the United Kingdom and Spain.

Limitations. ACLED captures violent events better than calm gatherings, which inflates the rates. Claims are classified by keywords in the event notes, with a margin of error. Repression and violence feed each other: the correlation does not say which comes first. Countries with small samples give unstable rates, hence the adjustable minimum threshold above. A family groups several countries, so its η² stays below that of the country.
